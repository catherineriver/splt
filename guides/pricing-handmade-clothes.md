# How to price handmade clothes

A price has to cover three things: the materials, your time, and the costs of running the work. Profit comes on top of those, not out of them.

![Handmade dresses on a wooden clothes rack](/guides/img/dress-rack.jpg)

## 1. Add up the cost of the make

- **Materials:** fabric by the length used, notions, the pattern or a share of it.
- **Your time:** every hour, including cutting, fitting, pressing and finishing, at an hourly rate you'd accept for the work.
- **Overheads:** what the work costs whether or not you're sewing: machine servicing, needles and blades, electricity, packaging, photos, and the fees a shop platform or payment service takes per sale.

Overheads are easy to forget. A simple way to cover them is to add a percentage to the materials and labour, for example 10–20%, and adjust once you know your own numbers.

## 2. Choose how to get from cost to price

- **Markup:** price = cost × (1 + markup). A 50% markup on a $139.40 cost gives $209.10.
- **Margin:** price = cost ÷ (1 − margin). A 50% margin on $139.40 gives $278.80, and half of every sale is profit.
- **Hourly rate first:** set the rate you need, then price = materials + overheads + hours × rate. Here the profit is already inside the rate.

Markup and margin at the same percentage give different prices. Pick one way and use it every time, so your prices stay consistent.

## A worked example

A linen shirt:

- Fabric: 2.2 m at $12/m = $26.40
- Notions: $9.00
- Pattern: $14.00
- Time: 6 hours at $15/hour = $90.00
- Cost: $139.40
- Overheads at 15%: $20.91
- **Full cost: $160.31**
- With a 40% margin: $160.31 ÷ 0.6 = **$267.18**, which you might round to $265 or $270.

## Check the price against the market

Look at what similar handmade pieces sell for, in your area or on the platforms you'd use. If your price is far above them, look at the hours first: a second shirt from the same pattern is usually faster than the first. If it's far below them, you may be underpricing your time.
